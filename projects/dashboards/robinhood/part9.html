Day trading — watchlist, backtest and the reason to keep it on paper

You asked for twenty to fifty names on a day-trade watchlist and for some simulated trades to see how they do. Here are twenty of the most-traded volatile names in the market, two mechanical strategies run over every one of them across 62 real sessions, and the results without any polish on them. Read the panel below first, because there is a hard constraint on this account that matters more than any of the numbers.

Before anything else: as of the Aug 12, 2026 close you are $17,954 above the pattern-day-trader floor. Your margin equity — securities value $91,314 minus the $48,360 margin debit — is $42,954 against the $25,000 minimum the rule requires to day trade in a margin account. That is a $17,954 cushion, or 71.8% of the floor — and note where it came from: two weeks ago it was a $662 shortfall, and the repair was the market, not the trading. If you place a fourth day trade inside five business days you are flagged as a pattern day trader permanently, and from that moment any dip below $25,000 — caused by your trading or simply by the market opening down — puts the account into closing-only restriction until you deposit enough to fix it. A single bad session does that. The risk of day trading here is not losing the trade; it is losing the account's ability to function. Separately, your overnight buying power is $1,390, which is less than one properly-sized position in most of the names below. This tab is a monitor. It is not a green light.

What the two strategies actually did — 2026-04-27 → 2026-07-24, 62 sessions

Strategy A — buy the opening-range breakout

Mark the high of the first 30 minutes. Buy if price clears it, stop at the range low, exit at the close. 552 trades. Win rate 43.8%. Average winner +1.69%, average loser -1.57%. Expectancy -0.15% per trade after costs, profit factor 0.83, summed return -84.2%. It stopped out 26.3% of the time and it lost 18 trades in a row at its worst. The raw edge before costs was already negative at -0.05%, so this is not a commission problem — the pattern itself does not work on this universe in this window.

Strategy B — fade the gap

When a stock opens more than 3% away from the prior close, bet on the gap closing. 212 trades. Win rate 39.6%. Expectancy -0.37% per trade, profit factor 0.76, summed return -79.0%, median trade -1.60%. Nearly half of them — 45.3% — hit the stop. SOXL alone contributed 48 of these trades and won 16.7% of them for a summed −66.6%. Fading gaps in leveraged ETFs is the single most expensive thing in this study.

Both strategies lose money, and I am not going to dress that up. Between them that is 764 simulated trades and not one positive expectancy. The honest caveat cuts the other way too: 62 sessions is a short sample and the results are not statistically significant, so this is not proof that day trading cannot work — it is proof that these two obvious mechanical patterns, on these twenty liquid names, in this window, did not. Which is roughly what you should expect, because both are the first ideas everyone tries and they are arbitraged accordingly. The reason to show you the losing backtest rather than hunt for a winning one is that finding a profitable rule in 62 sessions of data is trivial and means nothing; I could produce one in ten minutes and it would be fitted to noise. Costs were charged at 0.10% round trip, which is generous to the strategies — real slippage on the cheaper names is worse.

How to read the columns. Average daily range is the typical high-to-low move as a percentage of price — it is the raw material of a day trade, and below roughly 2% there is not enough movement to pay for the spread. SPY sits at 0.97% and is on the list purely as a benchmark for what “calm” looks like. Opening-range width is how much of that move happens in the first thirty minutes; a wide opening range means the day's decision gets made before most people have finished their coffee. The two strategy columns are per-trade expectancy for that symbol alone — and the trade counts are small, mostly ten to forty, so treat every per-symbol number as noise and do not pick names off this ranking. The last column is the one that bites: at 0.5% of equity risked per trade with a stop one daily range away, that is the largest position the arithmetic allows, and only 0 of the twenty fit inside your $1,390 of buying power. Sizing smaller than that does not fix it — it just makes the position too small for the profit to matter after costs.

What I would actually do with this tab. Keep it, watch it, and let it run empty for a while. The useful next step is not a first real trade — it is thirty sessions of writing down the trade you would have taken and what it would have done, because that costs nothing and it is the only way to find out whether you have an edge or an urge. If you want a rule for when this stops being paper: the PDT cushion needs to be several thousand dollars, the margin debit needs to be materially smaller, and a strategy needs positive expectancy over more than the 62 sessions we have here. The first is now true — the cushion is $17,954, against a $662 shortfall three weeks ago. The other two are not, and the first one got there because one position printed +29%, not because anything about the account got safer. A cushion the market handed you in a session is a cushion the market can take back in a session. The most profitable day-trading decision available to you right now is the one where you don't.
