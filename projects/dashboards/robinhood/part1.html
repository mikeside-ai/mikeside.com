Read the last column first. At a 30% house maintenance requirement you have $382 of room today, and every scenario in that table except the mildest puts you through it. Robinhood’s published maintenance requirement is 25% for most equities, but house requirements are raised at the broker’s discretion on concentrated, volatile or recently-halted names — which describes a large share of this book — and they are raised precisely when volatility spikes, which is to say the week of an earnings gap. The 25% column is the legal floor; the 30% column is the realistic one. Neither triggers on paper losses alone: a call is met by depositing cash or by the broker selling your positions for you, at its timing rather than yours.

The recommendation, plainly: raise about $9,000 against the debit before Monday’s close, and take it from the winners. RKLB ($11,720, +42%), HOOD ($3,144, +89%), PL ($2,920, +299%), BP ($2,166, +36%), TSM ($1,873, +143%), KSS ($762, +77%) — $23,819 of gains you have already made. Selling $9,000 of that does not raise your margin equity by a cent, and I want to be precise about that because it is the most misunderstood thing on this page: the shares become cash, the cash pays down the debit, and $24,338 stays $24,338. What it buys is distance. The debit falls to $46,897, the 25% call level drops to $62,529 so your cushion widens from 7.1% to 12.2%, the 30% cushion goes from $382 to $4,239, and you stop paying about $432 a year of interest on money you did not need to borrow. Do it before Aug 3, not after, because the entire point is to be holding less borrowed exposure when the binary events land.

The case against trimming — stated fairly

It deserves to be said. RKLB is already 42% from your cost and it reports Monday; names that are down going into a print are exactly the ones that gap up on a relief rally, and if you sell a third of it Friday and it opens +15% Tuesday you will feel it. That is a real cost and I am not going to pretend it is not. Two things settle it anyway. First, the asymmetry is not symmetric: a good print recovers paper losses you can wait for, while a bad print on 3.3× leverage can force a sale at a price you did not choose. Second, trimming the winners rather than RKLB itself sidesteps the whole dilemma — you keep the full recovery position and still cut the borrowing. That is why the list above is winners first.
