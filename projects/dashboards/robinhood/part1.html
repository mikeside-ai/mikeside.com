Read the last column first. At a 30% house maintenance requirement you have $20,681 of room today, and every scenario in that table except the mildest puts you through it. Robinhood’s published maintenance requirement is 25% for most equities, but house requirements are raised at the broker’s discretion on concentrated, volatile or recently-halted names — which describes a large share of this book — and they are raised precisely when volatility spikes, which is to say the week of an earnings gap. The 25% column is the legal floor; the 30% column is the realistic one. Neither triggers on paper losses alone: a call is met by depositing cash or by the broker selling your positions for you, at its timing rather than yours.

The recommendation, plainly: be clear about which risk you are buying down, because trimming winners and protecting the floor are two different trades. RKLB ($15,170, +83%), NVDA ($9,796, +29%), NOW ($10,176, +40%), LUNR ($3,703, +98%), PL ($2,828, +404%), HOOD ($2,394, +55%) — $44,067 of market value in names you are up on, and a thinner list than a month ago because you have already harvested most of it. Raising $6,000 from that list does not lift your margin equity by a cent, and I want to be precise about that because it is the most misunderstood number on this page: the shares become cash, the cash pays down the debit, and $41,518 stays $41,518. What it buys is distance from a call. The debit falls to $42,621, the 25% call level drops to $56,828 so the cushion widens from 28.1% to 32.5%, the 30% cushion goes from $20,681 to $23,252, and you stop paying about $288 a year in interest on money you did not need to borrow. All of that is worth doing and I would do it, and doing it now — with the debit at $48,621 and the book still up 15.4% on cost — is a great deal cheaper than being forced into it at July’s prices. It is also the honest alternative to funding new positions with borrowed money: if you want a new position, sell something you like less, do not borrow another $1,379. What it barely touches is the 18.3% floor threshold — unless the names you trim are the ones with dates. NVDA is on that winner list, so trimming it does double duty: it cuts the debit and shrinks the Aug 26 tail at the same time. KULR is not, and its Aug 13 print has already passed — it is 66% below your cost, and the only way to shrink that loss now is to book it — real money, and a real tax asset. Both choices are defensible. Doing neither is now defensible in a way it was not a month ago — the floor is $16,518 away and no single print can reach it. What is not defensible is doing neither while also adding leverage. Standing still is a position; borrowing more to buy more, with 16% of a 2.2×-levered book still carrying dates, is a different decision and a worse one.

The case against trimming — stated fairly

It deserves to be said. KULR is already 66% below your cost; names that go into a print already beaten down are exactly the ones that gap up on a relief rally, and if you cut a third of a deep loser and it rips +15% you will feel that for a long time. It is a real cost and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Two things settle it anyway. First, the payoff is not symmetric: a good print recovers paper losses you can simply wait for, while a bad print on 2.2× leverage can force a sale at a price you did not choose. Second, trimming the winners rather than a deep loser sidesteps the dilemma entirely — you keep the whole recovery position and still cut the borrowing. That is why the list above is winners first, and why the honest answer to “how much” is that it matters less than “which”.
