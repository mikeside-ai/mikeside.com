Read the last column first. At a 30% house maintenance requirement you have $5,058 of room today, and every scenario in that table except the mildest puts you through it. Robinhood’s published maintenance requirement is 25% for most equities, but house requirements are raised at the broker’s discretion on concentrated, volatile or recently-halted names — which describes a large share of this book — and they are raised precisely when volatility spikes, which is to say the week of an earnings gap. The 25% column is the legal floor; the 30% column is the realistic one. Neither triggers on paper losses alone: a call is met by depositing cash or by the broker selling your positions for you, at its timing rather than yours.

The recommendation, plainly: be clear about which risk you are buying down, because trimming winners and protecting the floor are two different trades. RKLB ($12,990, +57%), PL ($2,560, +356%), LUNR ($2,468, +32%), HOOD ($2,164, +40%), BP ($904, +54%), VSAT ($768, +754%) — $23,210 of gains you have already made, and a thinner list than a week ago because you have already harvested most of it. Raising $6,000 from that list does not lift your margin equity by a cent, and I want to be precise about that because it is the most misunderstood number on this page: the shares become cash, the cash pays down the debit, and $27,187 stays $27,187. What it buys is distance from a call. The debit falls to $45,634, the 25% call level drops to $60,845 so the cushion widens from 12.7% to 16.4%, the 30% cushion goes from $5,058 to $7,629, and you stop paying about $288 a year in interest on money you did not need to borrow. All of that is worth doing and I would do it. What it barely touches is the 2.8% floor threshold — unless the names you trim are the ones with dates. RKLB is on that winner list, so trimming it does double duty: it cuts the debit and shrinks the Aug 10 tail at the same time. PLTR is not. It reports Aug 3, it is 23% below your cost, and the only way to shrink that particular tail is to book the loss — real money, and a real tax asset. Both choices are defensible. Doing neither, and carrying 30.5% of a 2.9×-levered book into two prints in eight days, is the one option the arithmetic does not support.

Aug 3–7 — how to behave

Three things. One: do not day trade. It is technically permitted again — margin equity is $2,187 above the floor now — and that is exactly why it is a trap. A fourth day trade inside five business days flags the account permanently, and once flagged, any dip below $25,000 freezes it into closing-only, including a dip you did not cause. Your cushion is 2.8% of the book in a week when 25.8% of it reports. Two: do not open new positions with the $3,366 of buying power. That is borrowed money, and adding leverage the week 25.8% of your book reports is the exact inverse of what the arithmetic says — the same objection applies to the NEM and XOM buys on Jul 30, which were sound picks funded the wrong way. Three: Friday Aug 7 is the jobs report at 8:30am, before the open. Whatever you intend to do that week, do it Thursday.

The case against trimming — stated fairly

It deserves to be said. PLTR is already 23% below your cost and it reports Aug 3; names that go into a print already beaten down are exactly the ones that gap up on a relief rally, and if you cut a third of it and it opens +15% you will feel that for a long time. It is a real cost and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Two things settle it anyway. First, the payoff is not symmetric: a good print recovers paper losses you can simply wait for, while a bad print on 2.9× leverage can force a sale at a price you did not choose. Second, trimming the winners rather than PLTR itself sidesteps the dilemma entirely — you keep the whole recovery position and still cut the borrowing. That is why the list above is winners first, and why the honest answer to “how much” is that it matters less than “which”.
