How the picks for nights 3–5 are made model v7 · backtested against #92 and #93

Every proposed improvement was tested and every one of them lost. Three plausible ideas — weight Secret Language songs, avoid the too-obvious picks, penalise long-dormant songs — were each turned into a mechanical rule and scored against the two nights already played. None beat plain frequency ranking. The slate below is unchanged as a result.

Secret Language: real, and already baked in

1994 setlist footnotes mention signals 591 times. Ranked, the Secret-Language-heavy songs are Sample in a Jar (16), Down with Disease (15), Run Like an Antelope (14), Poor Heart (13), Sparkle (12), Hold Your Head Up (12), David Bowie (12) — the same songs frequency ranking already picks. Signals happen inside the band's biggest vehicles, so Secret Language is collinear with play count. Filtering on it returns an identical slate and an identical score. It's an excellent lens for watching a show; it is not a selection signal.

“Not too on-the-nose”: right about the calls, wrong about the slate

Turned into a rule — skip the most obvious picks — it loses hits every way it's cut: skip-top-3 costs three, mid-depth costs two, skip-top-5 costs one. The band does play its most-played songs.

Where the instinct is correct is the four called shots, and Jul 24 is the evidence. The obvious vacuum call didn't come, plausibly because Fish's novelty slot was already spent on Hold Your Head Up on night 1; and the obvious a cappella gave way to a less predictable one. Being clever pays on specific novelty picks and costs on the bulk slate.

Dormancy: do not filter on it

Measured leak-free — using each song's gap at the moment it was played, taken from the 2026 rows themselves rather than its current gap, which resets for every song this run has already burned and would have made a dormancy filter look fraudulently good:

Both nights are built identically: a current-rotation core plus 3–4 genuine deep bustouts. The median song was played about ten shows ago, but roughly a fifth of each night is vault material. So a dormancy penalty would be actively wrong — it would throw away 20% of the show. Equally, the bustouts are unpickable: 3–4 land out of ~50 plausible candidates, which puts any specific vault call under 10%. Both halves of that intuition are confirmed as descriptions of what the band does, and neither improves a 20-song slate.

The consequence for the called shots is blunt: Bustout is close to an unwinnable category as currently defined. Better framings for the next version are to predict the number of 300-plus-gap songs — 3–4 is a tight range hit twice now — or to call the category (a cappella, bluegrass, vacuum bit) rather than the title.

The model, in full

1. Count plays in the theme year. 2. Strike every song already played in this run. 3. Take the top 20. The no-repeat rule in step 2 is the only real insight, and it's free — 7/22 and 7/24 share zero songs, and 50 are off the board tonight.

One idea deliberately not acted on

Category no-repeat. If song types spread across a run the way individual songs do, then night 2 spending the a cappella slot argues against Amazing Grace tonight. The evidence is one occurrence across two nights — far too thin. It is queued for testing after #94 rather than applied now, because acting on untested hunches is exactly what produced the model this one replaced.

The honest caveat

Two scored nights is still a tiny sample, and 16/40 against a 7.7 baseline is encouraging rather than established. Expect scores to drift upward through the run regardless, because the eligible pool shrinks every night whether or not the model is any good.

Set placement is cosmetic. The slate predicts which twenty songs, not the running order. The four called shots are scored separately and strictly.
