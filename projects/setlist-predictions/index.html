How the picks for nights 3–5 are made model v6 · rebuilt 7/25

The v5 numbers on this page were wrong, and v6 exists because of it. v5 advertised 19/40 “held out.” That was a backtest artifact — the rules were tuned against nights 1 and 2 and then scored against those same two nights. The only genuine blind test this project has ever run is the published 1993 slate, and it scored 6 of 20.

For that night the pool was 83 songs and 24 got played, so twenty random picks expect 5.8. The model performed at chance. Everything below is the attempt to fix that honestly rather than re-grade the same two shows until the number looks good.

What v5 got right, and v6 keeps

Run no-repeat. Phish doesn't repeat inside a residency, and the two played nights confirm it absolutely — 7/22 and 7/24 have zero songs in common. Every confirmed song is struck from every later night's pool. This is the single most valuable rule in the model and it costs nothing.

Don't crowd the top of the frequency ranking. Both real setlists drew mostly from mid-depth rotation, with only about 7 of ~25 songs coming from that year's top 20.

What v5 got wrong

1. It banned era-appropriate covers. v5 calculated a cover slot at 0.10 expected hits against 0.32 for a rotation slot and cut them entirely. Then night 2 played six covers out of 24 songs — La Grange, Sparks, Good Times Bad Times, Daniel Saw the Stone, Sweet Adeline, Also Sprach Zarathustra. Night 1 played three. The mistake was treating “covers” as one enormous flat pool. The handful of covers that defined a specific year's rotation are neither flat nor rare, and v6 budgets three slots for them.

2. It drew the backwards lesson from night 2. v5 concluded the band played “deep-but-ordinary rotation, so avoid bustouts.” Night 2 was in fact a bustout showcase: Big Ball Jam (1,170-show gap), La Grange (531), Sweet Adeline (358), Sparks (357), and the first Kung since 2019. The real lesson isn't fewer bustouts — it's that the bustouts are era-signature novelty and covers, not Gamehendge set pieces. v5's night-3 slate was built on the backwards reading and has been rebuilt.

3. It ignored song families. Night 1 played Mike's Song → I Am Hydrogen → Weekapaug Groove and The Horse → Silent in the Morning. Pick one member of an inseparable pair and the rest are nearly free hits. v5 picked neither pair intact.

4. It ranked by frequency instead of filling roles. Both real nights decompose the same way — roughly 5 jam vehicles, 6 era staples, 7 short connective/novelty items, 4 era covers, 2 deep bustouts. A pure frequency ranking systematically under-picks that short connective layer because those songs are brief and score low on every “importance” proxy, yet they're about 30% of the setlist. v6 fills the five buckets separately.

The three biggest changes to tonight's slate

Tweezer was on the 1995 night and is now the #2 pick for tonight. 1994 is the single most Tweezer-defining year in the band's history — the Bomb Factory version, Tweezerfest — and it is unburned. Wolfman's Brother moved off the 1996 night for the same reason: it's a Hoist track, and Hoist is the 1994 record. And the whole era-cover layer — Loving Cup, Frankenstein, the Purple Rain vacuum solo — was absent from v5's slate entirely and is now budgeted.

The honest caveat

One real blind test, scored at chance, is the entire evidentiary base. v6's edge over random is about 1.8 songs out of 20, which a single night cannot confirm or refute — the run would need many more shows to distinguish that from noise. Expected scores also climb every night for a reason that has nothing to do with the model getting smarter: the eligible pool shrinks as songs get burned. Judge v6 on nights 3, 4 and 5 together, and treat any single-night result as close to meaningless.
