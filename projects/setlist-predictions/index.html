Phish @ MSG — Setlist Predictions

5 NIGHTS · #92–96

Five-night Madison Square Garden run celebrating Phish's 92nd through 96th shows at the Garden — each night themed to a year, 1992 → 1996. Each night has the twenty-song slate, four called shots, and a weighted ten-pick ladder for the setlist game at the bottom. Every pick shows theme-year plays × and current gap — 73× · g5 means played 73 times that year and last played 5 shows ago — so recently-played songs are obvious at a glance. Gaps over 50 are highlighted. Everything is editable; predictions never reuse a song played earlier in the run.

How the picks for nights 3–5 are made model v7 · backtested against #92 and #93

Every proposed improvement was tested and every one of them lost. Three plausible ideas — weight Secret Language songs, avoid the too-obvious picks, penalise long-dormant songs — were each turned into a mechanical rule and scored against the two nights already played. None beat plain frequency ranking. The 20-song slate is unchanged as a result.

Reading the gap numbers, and why low gaps are fine

Every pick carries its current gap so you can see instantly whether it's a current-rotation song or vault material. It's reasonable to worry that a song played five shows ago is “used up” — the data says otherwise. Measured leak-free, using each song's gap at the moment it was played (taken from the 2026 rows themselves, not its current gap, which resets for every song this run has already burned and would have made a dormancy filter look fraudulently good):

NightMedian gap75th pctMaxGap > 300
#92 — 27 songs10821,4683
#93 — 25 songs11881,1704

Both nights are built the same way: a current-rotation core plus 3–4 genuine deep bustouts. The typical song on a themed night was played about ten shows earlier, so a low gap is the norm rather than a warning sign. A dormancy penalty would be actively wrong — it would throw away the fifth of the show that is vault material — but the bustouts are also unpickable: 3–4 land out of ~50 plausible candidates, putting any specific vault call under 10%. The consequence for the called shots is blunt: Bustout is close to an unwinnable category as defined. Better framings are to predict the number of 300-plus-gap songs, or to call the category rather than the title.

The probabilities are calibrated, which was not guaranteed

Before building the ten-pick ladder, the raw play rate — theme-year count divided by that year's show count — was checked against reality. Summing those rates across the top 20 predicts expected hits, comparable to what actually happened:

#92 (1992)8.779

Combined ratio—0.93

A ratio of 0.93 means raw play rate is already a well-calibrated probability — no fudge factor needed. That matters more for the ladder than the slate, because the ladder needs probabilities right in magnitude, not just rank order.

Why the ladder is ranked strictly by probability

The setlist game scores Σ points × P(played). By the rearrangement inequality that sum is maximised by pairing the largest point value with the largest probability, and so on down. There is no cleverness available: the ranking is the entire strategy, and it is strictly by probability. Any deviation — saving a favourite for a high slot, hedging a risky pick low — provably loses expected points. One consequence worth internalising: 10 + 9 + 8 = 27 of the 55 available points sit in the top three slots, so half the game is decided by three songs.

A worked example: how Tweezer got onto the ladder

The single-night rate says Tweezer is 29% tonight — eleventh, off the ladder. But Tweezer is the kind of song worth analysing across the run rather than one show, because the no-repeat rule means it can only land once. Its rate falls across the three remaining theme years:

NightYear rateFirst-occurrenceIf played at all
N3 — 199429.0%29.0%48.9%
N4 — 199526.5%18.8%31.8%
N5 — 199621.9%11.4%19.3%
Never, across all three—40.8%—

Tonight is Tweezer's best single night of the three, and it gets worse from here — the theme years are progressively less Tweezer-heavy, so the “they're saving it” intuition runs backwards. Conditioning on it being played at all doesn't change that ordering; it only changes the magnitude.

Is certainty warranted? Modern data says no: 41 Tweezers in 252 shows since 2021, a 16.3% per-show rate, giving P(at least one) of 48% across any three consecutive shows, 62% across four, 75% across five. Two nights of this run have already passed without one. So the defensible range for “Tweezer appears in N3–N5” is 50–75%, putting tonight somewhere between 24% and 37% — not 49%.

The ladder uses 37%, the top of that range. Higher than the raw era rate because a five-night Garden residency is not an average 1994 show and the no-repeat burn keeps concentrating probability onto unplayed marquee vehicles; short of treating it as a certainty the data doesn't support. That slots Tweezer sixth and drops Fee (32%) off the bottom.

And the honest footnote: this decision is worth less than one point. Expected points move from 24.82 to 24.91. Had Tweezer been forced to third at 49% it would be 25.76 if that estimate is right and 24.16 if it's wrong — an upside of +0.94 against a downside of −0.66, on a 55-point scale. Worth getting directionally right; not worth agonising over.

Secret Language: real, and already baked in

1994 setlist footnotes mention signals 591 times. Ranked, the Secret-Language-heavy songs are Sample in a Jar (16), Down with Disease (15), Run Like an Antelope (14), Poor Heart (13), Sparkle (12), Hold Your Head Up (12), David Bowie (12) — the same songs frequency ranking already picks. Signals happen inside the biggest vehicles, so Secret Language is collinear with play count. Filtering on it returns an identical slate and an identical score. An excellent lens for watching a show; not a selection signal.

“Not too on-the-nose”: right about the calls, wrong about the slate

Turned into a rule — skip the most obvious picks — it loses hits every way it's cut: skip-top-3 costs three, mid-depth costs two, skip-top-5 costs one. The band does play its most-played songs. Where the instinct is correct is the four called shots, and Jul 24 is the evidence: the obvious vacuum call didn't come, plausibly because Fish's novelty slot was already spent on Hold Your Head Up on night 1, and the obvious a cappella gave way to a less predictable one. Being clever pays on specific novelty picks and costs on the bulk slate.

A correlation the slate doesn't handle well

The 20-song slate carries both Tweezer and Tweezer Reprise. In 1994 the Reprise appeared in roughly 69% of shows that had a Tweezer, so those two slots are very nearly one bet held twice. The same is true of Swept Away and Steep on the 1996 night. Left in place for now — the slate is deliberately frozen for head-to-head scoring against v5 and v6 — but decorrelating it is a real, cheap improvement for a future version.

The model, in full

1. Count plays in the theme year. 2. Strike every song already played in this run. 3. Take the top 20 for the slate; take the top 10 in strict probability order for the ladder. The no-repeat rule in step 2 is the only real insight, and it's free — 50 songs are off the board tonight.

One idea deliberately not acted on

Category no-repeat. If song types spread across a run the way individual songs do, then night 2 spending the a cappella slot argues against Amazing Grace tonight. The evidence is one occurrence across two nights — far too thin. Queued for testing after #94.

The honest caveat

Two scored nights is still a tiny sample, and 16/40 against a 7.7 baseline is encouraging rather than established. Expect scores to drift upward through the run regardless, because the eligible pool shrinks every night whether or not the model is any good.
