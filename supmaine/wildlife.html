Field guide · fauna

🦭 What's alive out there

The animals you have a real chance of seeing, day by day, and where to look. ★ = the one worth actively watching for that day. Everything here is keyed to early August on the mid-coast — which changes the list more than you'd think. Some famous Maine animals are simply not available to you this week, and those are called out honestly at the bottom rather than padding the list. Tap any photo for the full Wikipedia entry.

Two rules do most of the work. First, tide beats time of day on this coast — seals haul out on ledges as the tide drops and swim off as it floods, so the low-tide windows already on the day cards are also the wildlife windows. Second, the hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset is when mammals move; midday in August is the deadest part of the day for everything except seabirds.
Tuesday, Aug 4
Sheepscot estuary · Wiscasset · Boothbay Harbor
Osprey Pandion haliaetus — the big white-bellied fish hawk that builds a messy stick platform on top of channel markers, pilings and dead pines. Early August is the loudest week of their year: the chicks have fledged but still follow the adults around screaming to be fed. They hunt by hovering, then hitting the water feet-first and shaking off mid-air like a dog. Maine's population came back from near-zero after DDT, and the estuary here is thick with them. Look: channel markers on the Sheepscot, the pilings at Boothbay Harbor
Harbor seal Phoca vitulina — Maine's default seal, dog-faced and mottled, hauled out in clumps on exposed ledges. August pups are already weaned and swimming. If you see a lone head bobbing 40 yards out watching you, that's this. Give hauled-out seals a wide berth — 150 ft is the federal guidance, and a spooked group stampeding into the water can injure itself. Look: ledges in Boothbay Harbor as the tide drops
American lobster Homarus americanus — you'll eat one at Red's before you see one alive, but the buoys are the real show. Every one of those painted floats is a licensed lobsterman's registered color pattern, unique to them, repeated on their boat's mast so wardens can match trap to owner from a distance. Once you know that, a harbor full of buoys reads like a phone book. Look: literally everywhere on the water
Double-crested cormorant Nannopterum auritum — the prehistoric-looking black bird standing on a rock with its wings spread wide. It's not showing off; its feathers aren't fully waterproof, which is the trade-off that lets it dive deep, so it has to air-dry between fishing trips.
Common eider Somateria mollissima — big sea duck. In August you'll see a brown female towing 15–20 ducklings that aren't all hers: eiders pool their broods into crèches guarded by a few "aunties." The males are the striking black-and-white ones, mostly further offshore by now.
Wednesday, Aug 5
Camden · Penobscot Bay · Rockland · Union
Bald eagle Haliaeetus leucocephalus — Penobscot Bay holds one of the densest concentrations in the eastern United States, and the Olad sail is the single best eagle opportunity of your week. Adults are unmistakable; the brown ones with white blotches are juveniles, which take four to five years to get the white head. Watch the tall dead pines on the islands — eagles perch where they can see, not where it's pretty. Look: from the schooner, and from the Mt Battie summit ledges
Harbor porpoise Phocoena phocoena — small, shy, and easy to miss because it does not leap. You get a slow dark triangle of dorsal fin rolling forward and gone, usually in twos and threes. Calm water and a quiet sailboat is exactly the right platform. If a fin comes up three times in a line, that's your porpoise. Look: off the bow of the Olad in flat water
Peregrine falcon Falco peregrinus — the Camden Hills cliffs are historic peregrine ground and the recovery here is one of the state's real conservation wins. Look for a compact, sharp-winged shape cutting fast along a cliff face — nothing else moves like that. By August the young are flying and the family hunts together noisily. Look: the cliff faces below Mt Battie and toward Maiden Cliff
Great black-backed gull Larus marinus — the largest gull in the world, and Rockland Breakwater is full of them alongside the smaller herring gulls. Nearly a 5½-foot wingspan, jet-black mantle, and genuinely predatory — they'll take ducklings and other gulls' chicks. Once you can pick them out of a mixed flock, gulls stop being "gulls." Look: the Rockland Breakwater, standing on the granite
Common loon Gavia immer — this is your inland night. Union sits in lake country, and the loon's wail carries a long way across still water after dark. The tremolo (the "crazy laugh") means it's agitated; the long wail is just contact. Step outside at the dome after sunset and listen. Listen: after dark at the Union dome
Turkey vulture Cathartes aura — the big soaring bird holding its wings in a shallow V and rocking side to side. Eagles soar dead flat; if it wobbles, it's a vulture. Common over the Mt Battie ridgeline on a warm afternoon.
Thursday, Aug 6
Acadia · Bar Harbor · Belfast
Peregrine falcon Falco peregrinus — Acadia's cliff nests (Precipice, Jordan Cliffs, Valley Cove) are the reason certain trails close each spring, and the reason the closures lift once the young fledge in late summer. Stand at the Precipice pullout with binoculars and you can often watch fledglings harassing each other along the cliff. The stoop is the fastest movement in the animal kingdom — over 200 mph. Look: the Precipice pullout on the Park Loop Road
Beaver Castor canadensis — genuinely likely if you take the e-bike option, because the carriage roads run right past their ponds and beavers come out at dusk. Beaver Dam Pond and Witch Hole Pond are the classic spots. Look for the V-wake with a brown head at the point of it; the tail-slap is a warning aimed at you. Look: Beaver Dam Pond & Witch Hole Pond, first and last light
Black guillemot Cepphus grylle — the most underrated bird on this trip. Small, sooty black, with a bold white wing patch and bright coral-red feet and mouth lining. It's an auk — a puffin relative — and unlike puffins it stays inshore all summer, so it's the one member of that family you can reliably see from land here. Look: the water off Bar Harbor and along the Ocean Path
Harbor & gray seal Phoca vitulina / Halichoerus grypus — Frenchman Bay has both. Gray seals are much bigger with a long straight Roman-nosed profile that gets them the nickname horsehead; harbor seals have a short concave dog face. Side by side the difference is obvious; alone, look at the nose.
Red squirrel Tamiasciurus hudsonicus — you will hear this before you see it: a long furious mechanical chatter from a spruce, aimed at you. Smaller and rustier than a gray squirrel, and the loudest animal in the park by a distance. The piles of shredded cone scales at the base of conifers are its middens.
Snowshoe hare Lepus americanus — brown in August, white in winter, huge back feet. Dawn and dusk on the carriage roads and trail edges. Acadia has no coyote-free refuge, so they're jumpy; you'll get a two-second look.
White-tailed deer — common and casual around Sieur de Monts and the roadsides at dusk, which is also your real driving hazard on the Park Loop Road and Route 3 after dark. Belfast to Freeport that night is prime deer hour. Slow down.
Herring gull — worth naming because of the popovers. The Jordan Pond House gulls and the Thunder Hole gulls are professionals. Do not put a plate down and look away.
Friday, Aug 7
Cape Elizabeth · Casco Bay · Peaks Island
Harbor seal, from the ferry — the Peaks Island run threads past ledges that seals use hard, and a boat is a far better seal platform than a shoreline because you get above the glare. Sit on the right side heading out for the best angle on the outer ledges and take the trip slowly rather than staring at your phone. Look: the 2:15p ferry out, ledges on the harbor side
Common tern Sterna hirundo — sharper and more elegant than any gull: forked tail, black cap, red-black bill, and a hunting style that's all hover-and-plunge. Casco Bay's island colonies are actively managed, and August has adults ferrying fish back to nearly-grown young. If a bird looks like a gull that went to ballet school, it's a tern. Look: over the water at Fort Williams and from the ferry
Monarch butterfly Danaus plexippus — early August is the last resident generation before the ones that fly to Mexico. They'll be on milkweed and seaside goldenrod along the Fort Williams cliff walk. The generation being laid this week is the one that makes the 2,500-mile trip — a butterfly that will outlive its parents' entire lifespans several times over. Look: the meadow edges at Fort Williams, Greenwood Garden on Peaks
Double-crested cormorant — the black birds on every ledge off the Cape. Their guano is what bleaches the tops of the offshore rocks white; that's how you spot a colony from a mile away.
Osprey — nest platforms are scattered around the Cape Elizabeth marshes and Casco Bay islands. Same show as Tuesday, better backdrop.
Saturday, Aug 8
The wedding · oceanfront, Cape Elizabeth · 3:30–10 PM
The practical one: greenhead flies. Mid-July to mid-August is peak season for salt-marsh greenheads on the southern Maine coast, they bite hard, they hunt in daylight rather than at dusk, and they are drawn to dark colors and movement. An oceanfront ceremony near any marsh at 4:00 PM is exactly their hour. This is worth a discreet travel-size repellent in a jacket pocket or clutch — not a bottle on the bar, just insurance. They fade fast once the sun drops.
Tree swallow & chimney swift — cocktail hour, 3:30 to 5:00, is when swallows work the field edges for insects at head height. Swifts are the ones that look like flying cigars with stiff, flickering wingbeats and never seem to glide. Free entertainment during the part of a wedding where you're holding a drink and nodding.
Little & big brown bat Myotis lucifugus / Eptesicus fuscus — as the reception lights come on, bats take over the airspace the swallows just left. Maine's little brown bat was gutted by white-nose syndrome and is now state-endangered, so a bat over the lawn is a better sighting than it used to be. They're eating the mosquitoes; be glad they're there.
Firefly — early August is the tail end of the season in Maine, but field edges and hedgerows will still have a scattering after full dark. Late reception, dark lawn, worth a look away from the lights.
Herring gull — outdoor hors d'oeuvres, oceanfront, 3:30 PM. You know how this goes.
Sunday, Aug 9
Portland · South Portland · the coastal loop
Migrating shorebirds — here's the thing most visitors miss: "fall" shorebird migration peaks in early August, not in fall. Semipalmated sandpipers, short-billed dowitchers and greater yellowlegs are already streaming south from the Arctic, and every mudflat you pass this weekend is a refueling stop. A flock of small brown birds working a flat at low tide is not background — those birds hatched above the tree line six weeks ago. Look: any exposed mudflat at low tide, Greenbelt marsh crossings
Snowy egret Egretta thula — bright white, black bill, black legs and absurd yellow feet, which it uses to stir the mud and flush prey. The larger all-white one with a yellow bill is a great egret. Both work the South Portland and Cape marshes.
Harbor seal at Spring Point Ledge — the ledge and breakwater there hold seals on a dropping tide, and it's an easy stop with a lighthouse attached.
Osprey — nest platforms along the Greenbelt and Scarborough side. Adults are still provisioning fledglings this week.
Woodchuck Marmota monax — the fat brown thing that waddles off a rail-trail verge. Unglamorous, but if you're on e-bikes you'll see several.
Monday, Aug 10
Old Port · Scarborough Marsh · Kennebunkport · Timber Point
Saltmarsh sparrow Ammospiza caudacuta — if the Option B ride happens, this is the most significant animal of your whole trip. It nests only in Atlantic salt marsh, times its nesting to the gap between monthly high tides, and is losing that gap to sea-level rise. Populations are falling fast enough that credible projections put it near extinction within a few decades. Orange-buff face, grey cheek patch, streaky breast, and it skulks — you'll get a flash as it drops back into the grass. Look: Scarborough Marsh, the tall Spartina away from the road
Glossy ibis Plegadis falcinellus — dark, long down-curved bill, and in good light it isn't black at all but iridescent bronze-green. Scarborough is one of the northernmost places they reliably breed. A genuinely tropical-looking bird in a Maine marsh. Look: Scarborough Marsh, wading in the shallow pannes
Willet Tringa semipalmata — a plain grey wader that looks like nothing until it opens its wings and flashes a bold black-and-white stripe, screaming its own name. The loudest and most obvious bird in the marsh once it moves.
Piping plover Charadrius melodus — federally threatened, nests on the southern Maine beaches, and the roped-off symbolic fencing you'll see at Timber Point and the Kennebunkport beaches is protecting them. Sand-colored, runs in short bursts and stops dead. Stay outside the fencing and keep well clear of chicks on the open sand — they don't fly yet. Look: Timber Point, roped beach sections
Scarborough Marsh itself — 3,100 acres, the largest salt marsh in Maine, and one of the state's top birding sites. The Audubon center on Pine Point Road rents canoes and is the standard way in. Worth an hour if the day has one.

What you won't see — honestly

Moose. Wrong part of the state and wrong week. Maine's moose are inland and north — Baxter, Rangeley, the Moosehead region — and the coastal strip you're on has effectively none. Anyone promising you a coastal moose is selling a T-shirt.
Puffins — unless you make a decision. Atlantic puffins are on offshore rocks like Eastern Egg Rock, reachable only by dedicated boat trips out of Boothbay Harbor, New Harbor or Bar Harbor. The birds leave the nesting islands around mid-August, so this week is genuinely the closing window for the season, and it costs a half day. It's the one wildlife thing on this list that requires a real trade-off against the itinerary — flagging it rather than deciding it.
Whales. Humpbacks, finbacks and minkes are out there, but they're 20+ miles offshore on the feeding banks. It's a 4–5 hour committed trip out of Bar Harbor or Boothbay, not something you glimpse from the Ocean Path. Harbor porpoise is your realistic cetacean.
Black bear. Maine has roughly 35,000 of them, more than any state east of the Mississippi, and you will not see one. They're inland, nocturnal and want nothing to do with you.
Warbler waves. Spring migration is the famous one. By August the songbirds have stopped singing, are in drab plumage, and are hard to find even for people who care a lot. Don't feel bad about the quiet woods.
The one piece of kit worth grabbing: a single small pair of binoculars for the three of you. Eagles from the Olad, peregrines at the Precipice, seals from the ferry and the whole Scarborough marsh are all binocular-scale, not naked-eye-scale. Camden and Bar Harbor both have shops that sell them, and anything 8x32 or 8x42 fits in the Jeep door pocket and turns four of these days from "there's a bird" into an actual sighting.
